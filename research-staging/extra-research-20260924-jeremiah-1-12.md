# Jeremiah textual-control rule — MT, Old Greek/LXX, Qumran and Ethiopic must be tracked together

Jeremiah requires stricter textual control than most books investigated so far.

## Major edition difference

The Old Greek/LXX Jeremiah is substantially shorter than the Masoretic Text and arranges some material differently.

Emanuel Tov and other textual scholars have argued that:
- the LXX translator often worked relatively literally;
- the shorter Greek therefore likely reflects a shorter Hebrew Vorlage rather than arbitrary translator abridgment;
- Qumran witnesses **4QJerᵇ** and **4QJerᵈ** preserve readings/order that in important places resemble the shorter Greek form;
- this supports the existence of more than one ancient Hebrew literary edition of Jeremiah.

Tov describes:
- a shorter edition represented by the Hebrew source behind the Greek and partly by 4QJerᵇ/ᵈ;
- a longer edition represented by MT;
- differences in both length and arrangement.

This model remains debated.
Jack Lundbom, for example, argued that many LXX omissions could instead reflect haplography in the Hebrew source behind the Greek.
More recent scholarship continues to debate whether:
- the shorter edition is earlier;
- the longer MT is secondary expansion;
- or the two developed in parallel from an earlier common form.

### Permanent rule for this project

For Jeremiah:

> **Do not treat MT-only material as automatically original, and do not treat LXX-shorter material as automatically earlier.**

For every high-value geometry/process text:
1. check MT;
2. check Old Greek/LXX;
3. check Qumran where extant;
4. note Ethiopic transmission when relevant.

---

## Ethiopic Jeremiah control

Recent Ethiopic scholarship notes that:
- Jeremiah has a substantial Geʿez manuscript tradition;
- unlike some Ethiopic biblical books, a full modern critical edition of Ethiopic Jeremiah has historically been lacking;
- current projects collating many manuscripts are reconstructing its textual history;
- broader Ethiopic Old Testament scholarship generally finds the earliest Geʿez translations derive primarily from Greek/Septuagint Vorlagen, with later revision and influence from other traditions.

For this 88-text project that means:

> **Greek-LXX differences are especially important when reconstructing the Ethiopian biblical reading environment.**

Do not simply use MT as the sole textual base.

# SECTION 50A textual / historical controls

1. Jeremiah's textual history is unusually pluriform; MT and Old Greek differ in length and arrangement.
2. 4QJerᵇ/ᵈ provide Hebrew evidence broadly compatible with a shorter Jeremiah tradition in some passages.
3. Scholarly models differ on whether the shorter and longer editions are sequential or parallel developments.
4. Ethiopic Jeremiah belongs to a Geʿez textual tradition whose earliest form is generally studied in relation to Greek/Septuagint Vorlage; later revision layers complicate reconstruction.
5. Jeremiah 1's almond image is explicitly interpreted through Hebrew wordplay as watchfulness/performance, not as a mechanical rod.
6. Jeremiah 1's boiling pot is explicitly interpreted as disaster from northern kingdoms; directional-flow comparison is secondary.
7. Jeremiah 1's city/pillar/wall body projection is metaphorical and therefore a methodological control on literal Squatter-Man readings.
8. Jeremiah 2's fountain/cistern imagery is grounded in ordinary water-source/storage experience.
9. Jeremiah 4:23–26 has strong scholarly support as creation-reversal imagery, but whether it depicts universal cosmic de-creation or Judah's land in creation language is debated.
10. “Not a full end” is critical and must remain attached to any Vardath reset comparison.
11. Jeremiah 6's refining imagery is grounded in real metallurgical concepts; precise technical reconstruction remains debated.
12. Jeremiah 7 demonstrates temple/gate access as theological/political rhetoric, not literal portal safety engineering.
13. Jeremiah 9's window-entry image is poetic personification of death but structurally useful for permeability analysis.
14. Jeremiah 10:6–8, 10 are absent from key shorter witnesses (Qumran/Greek noted in modern editions); textual status must be controlled before using those lines heavily.
15. Jeremiah 10:20's tent/cord imagery remains high-value even as poetic national-household metaphor.
16. Jeremiah 12 returns to the book's programmatic pluck-up/build-up grammar.
