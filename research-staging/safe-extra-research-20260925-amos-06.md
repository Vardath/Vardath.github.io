# SECTION 56C6 textual / historical / scholarly controls

1. Working base text: Amos 6 NRSVUE.
2. Zion and Mount Samaria are historical/political/religious centers; their pairing is a critique of elite security, not evidence of upper/lower world polarity.
3. Calneh, Hamath and Gath function as comparative political/geographic examples. Exact historical circumstances and dating of their defeats can be debated; the secure textual function is external comparison.
4. Amos 6:3 contrasts “put far away the evil day” with “bring near a reign/seat of violence.” Treat as moral-political temporal irony, not literal manipulation of time.
5. Amos 6:4–7 is widely read as critique of elite luxury and indifference to Joseph/Israel’s ruin. The issue is the disconnect between comfort and system suffering.
6. Ivory furniture is materially plausible in the Iron Age Samaria elite environment; archaeological ivory evidence supports context, not identification of a specific bed.
7. “First to exile” reverses elite priority and predicts displacement. It does not specify a physical transport mechanism.
8. Amos 6:9–10 describes severe mortality in a house and post-death removal. NRSVUE marks part of 6:10’s Hebrew corpse/burning terminology as uncertain. Do not infer exact funerary technology.
9. The command to hush/not mention YHWH has several interpretive possibilities; the secure process is speech/invocation suppression under catastrophic conditions.
10. Great-house/little-house shattering establishes scale breadth of structural failure but does not identify earthquake, siege, pressure wave or other force by itself.
11. Amos 6:12’s horse/crag and ox/plow/sea questions are rhetorical impossible/inappropriate-operation analogies. They are exceptionally useful as methodological controls for medium compatibility.
12. Amos 6:12 repeats Amos 5’s justice/wormwood inversion but adds poison and “fruit of righteousness,” emphasizing actively harmful output rather than mere absence.
13. Lo-debar and Karnaim are treated in many translations/scholarly discussions as real Transjordanian locations whose Hebrew names also enable wordplay. Lo-debar can be heard as “nothing/no thing”; Karnaim evokes “horns,” a strength image.
14. Yoon Jong Yoo, “Wordplay and Amos’s Judgment Oracles,” specifically analyzes Amos 6:13–14 as wordplay that undercuts Israel’s boasting.
15. Historical reconstructions often connect Israelite control of Lo-debar/Karnaim with territorial recovery east of the Jordan, but the wordplay’s rhetorical function does not require certainty about every military episode.
16. Lebo-hamath and Wadi Arabah function as broad boundary markers; many translations explain 6:14 as oppression from the northern to southern extent of Israel’s territory.
17. External nation is a political/military agent in the chapter. No inter-world transfer is described.
18. Strict fingertrap status remains negative.
