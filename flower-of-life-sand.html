Experiment controls

Geometry: Each full-sized circular disc has independent clockwise surface motion. In overlapping regions the discs are modeled as conceptual superposed layers, not as interpenetrating solid hardware.

Hypothesis: Can repeated overlapping clockwise motion concentrate randomly supplied sediment into large, contiguous regions, rather than only local circular patterns?

How to read this: Gold shows accumulated sediment mass; the bright contour marks a user-chosen height threshold. The largest connected deposit is measured on the thresholded map, not assumed to be a continent.

Controls: Set RPM to zero for a control experiment. Vary the threshold and try extended simulation times using +5 simulated seconds.

Limits: This is a 2D, idealized transport/deposition experiment. The overlapping discs are mathematically superposed rotating surfaces, not possible solid hardware. This is not a calibrated DEM, erosion model, or evidence of geographic continent formation.

Continental-form hypothesis

The question is whether a hexagonal lattice of full overlapping clockwise rotations can spontaneously redistribute uniformly supplied sediment into persistent, broad, interconnected patches with irregular boundaries. No continents, coastline shapes, or target outcome are programmed into the initial sand distribution.

Interpretation: A contour is a thresholded sand-height map, not an actual shoreline. The system is spatially periodic, so repeated flower motifs may be the genuine outcome. Compare with a stationary (0 RPM) control before attributing any large-scale structure to rotation. This simplified coupled-field numerical experiment omits grain collisions, topography, gravity-driven avalanches and 3D granular mechanics.
