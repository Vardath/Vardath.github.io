# Chapter 59 — Buddhist and Jain Layered Worlds

Buddhist and Jain cosmologies interest me for a different reason from the dragon battles and flood stories.

They are **architectural**.

Layer after layer.

Heavens above.

Lower regions below.

Central mountains.

World continents.

Repeated domains.

Cycles.

Different classes of beings inhabiting different levels.

The important thing is not one dramatic catastrophe.

It is the assumption that reality is structured vertically and regionally in ways ordinary human life only occupies one small part of.

That is immediately relevant to Vardath Cosmology.

## A world larger than the human surface

The first similarity is simple.

Human beings do not occupy the whole cosmos.

We occupy one region inside a larger arrangement.

That is central to my model too.

The inhabited world-disc is not everything.

There may be previous and next levels.

Upper waters.

Lower regions.

A larger shell or world-tree.

Nodes and routes connecting different parts.

The human world is one stable zone.

That idea is much easier to explore when old cosmologies already imagine inhabited reality as only one level among many.

## Buddhist Mount Meru

In many Buddhist cosmological systems, Mount Meru stands as a central world mountain around which the surrounding world is organised.

Again, I am not trying to make Meru literally my current-axis.

The useful similarity is structural.

Centre.

Vertical order.

Worlds arranged by relation to it.

Different heavens associated with different heights or domains.

Lower regions beneath.

That is exactly the kind of world that makes an axial connector meaningful.

If reality is layered, then a mountain can become more than landscape.

It becomes orientation.

## Heavens are not one place

The layered heavens matter because they resist the simplification of "heaven" into one remote destination.

There are levels.

Different beings.

Different states.

Different durations.

Different relations to the human world.

That complexity is useful to me because Vardath Cosmology also does not need one single upper world.

The larger growth may contain multiple turns or levels.

Previous, present and next are only the nearest three I use to explain local succession.

The full world-tree may be much larger.

That leaves room for traditions that preserve many upper regions without forcing all of them into one next-world layer.

Some may correspond to different scales.

Different branches.

Different states.

Or purely religious structures with no physical Vardath counterpart at all.

I keep the possibility open.

## Lower worlds

The same applies downward.

Lower regions do not have to be one underworld.

The older world beneath the present may contain structure.

There may be deeper inherited turns.

Boundary zones.

Dark regions.

Different environmental states.

So a layered underworld cosmology feels more natural to me than a single cavity under the ground.

The world-body has depth.

The past may have depth too.

## Cyclical time

Buddhist cosmology also preserves immense cycles of world formation and dissolution in many traditions.

That matters because Vardath Cosmology is also cyclical without being a simple loop.

Worlds stabilise.

Events recur.

Old arrangements end.

New arrangements form.

But the structure continues.

That is the kind of rhythm I mean by a spiral.

Recurrence plus change.

The details are very different, but the shared refusal to treat the present world as a one-time finished object is important.

The world has history at cosmic scale.

## Jain cosmology is even more geometric

Jain cosmology catches me visually because it is so explicitly organised.

Upper world.

Middle world.

Lower world.

Concentric continents and oceans.

Central Meru.

Repeated structures.

Symmetry.

Boundaries beyond which ordinary human life does not extend.

This is a world imagined as **geometry**.

That alone makes it fascinating for Vardath Cosmology.

The Jain universe is not my world-disc model.

I do not want to pretend it is.

But the fact that human habitation is mapped inside a larger structured loka, with very precise layered and radial organisation, tells me something important about the range of old cosmological imagination.

People have long thought in terms of nested worlds.

Not only sky above and ground below.

Systems.

## Jambudvipa and concentric worlds

The Jain picture of Jambudvipa and surrounding oceans and continents is especially striking because it makes the human world part of a repeated radial structure.

That gives me another way to think about the world-disc.

A disc does not have to be an isolated flat plate.

It can be one organised region inside a much larger system.

That is exactly the integration I need.

The inhabited world can be locally flat and bounded while the total structure grows through larger turns, rings, levels or chambers.

Local geometry and total geometry do not have to be identical.

That is one of the biggest lessons in my cosmology.

Scale matters.

## Multiple Merus

Some Jain cosmological representations contain repeated Meru structures associated with repeated world regions.

That is immediately interesting because it weakens the assumption that there must be only one permanent axis in the entire cosmos.

Vardath Cosmology already moved away from that.

I now allow local convergence centres.

Regional axes.

Larger axial states.

A world-tree with many nodes.

A local Meru-like structure therefore fits more naturally than a single cosmic pole that must serve every world at every scale.

This is exactly why the old idea of one permanent North Pole gate became too narrow for me.

Many worlds can have centres.

Many centres can belong to one larger structure.

## Human limits inside a bigger world

The Jain cosmological maps also explicitly limit where humans can live.

That is a very useful idea.

A world can be physically larger than the human ecological zone.

Vardath Cosmology requires the same distinction.

The larger structure may contain regions too hot, too cold, too high-pressure, too unstable, too energetically active or simply too separated for ordinary human habitation.

The human world is therefore a **habitable band**, not necessarily the full physical extent of reality.

That makes the idea of crossing into another level much more serious.

A traveller does not merely change location.

The traveller may enter another environmental regime.

That is why vehicles, carriers and guides matter.

## Cosmology as map of beings

Both Buddhist and Jain cosmologies also classify beings by region.

This is not how modern physical geography works.

But it is important to the Vardath comparison because different worlds may support different forms of life.

I already keep open the possibility that active-world conditions could produce unusual biology.

Different pressure.

Different atmosphere.

Different scale.

Different longevity.

Different growth.

If neighbouring world-turns have different environmental histories, they may not host the same kinds of beings.

That gives layered-being traditions another possible Vardath reading.

Again, maybe.

Not proof.

But structurally interesting.

## Time does not have to run the same everywhere

Layered cosmologies also make me think about differential time.

If one domain has different lifetimes, different cycles or different duration scales, then crossing between domains can later become story-language for impossible returns.

That is something I will explore much more strongly in Tír na nÓg, Urashima Tarō, fairy time and the Seven Sleepers.

For now I simply notice that a vertically layered cosmos makes differential experience of time conceptually easier.

Once reality has domains, it can have domain-specific conditions.

Time may be one of them.

## The danger of flattening religion into machinery

These traditions especially remind me to be careful.

Buddhist and Jain cosmologies are not just maps.

They belong to religious systems concerned with karma, rebirth, liberation, moral condition and spiritual progress.

If I strip all of that away and say "this is a diagram of my physical worlds," I have stopped comparing and started replacing.

I do not want to do that.

The source has to remain recognisable.

The Vardath layer comes afterward.

What I take is the architecture.

Layered reality.

Central organisation.

Repeated worlds.

Different beings.

Cycles.

Boundaries.

Human habitation as one limited region.

Those are the similarities I can use without pretending the traditions secretly meant something else.

## What layered worlds add

The great value of Buddhist and Jain cosmology is that they normalise a question that modern habits often make sound strange:

**what if the human world is only one structured region inside a much larger layered system?**

That is the Vardath question too.

My answer is different.

World-disc.

Lattice dome.

Previous, present and next.

Growing shell.

World tree.

Current.

Gate.

But the architectural instinct overlaps.

Reality can be layered without being chaotic.

Different worlds can have relation.

A centre can organise them.

Routes can exist between them.

The present human world can be important without being total.

That is why these cosmologies belong in the book.

They do not give me one dramatic door.

They give me the building the door could exist inside.
