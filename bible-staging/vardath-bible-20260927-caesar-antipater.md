### The Rise of Caesar

The account adds that Pompey the Great had earlier subdued those lands and compelled them to obey Rome.

When he went away toward the eastern regions, they later rebelled.

At that time there was in Rome a man named Caesar who was numbered among the three hundred and twenty rulers and counsellors of the state.

He went out toward the eastern lands and made war there.

He overcame twenty-two kings and many cities.

The Jews also submitted to him because of the greatness of his power and the strength of his armies.

When Caesar returned, he would no longer accept the old restraints that had been laid upon the rulers of Rome.

He set aside former oaths and agreements and rejected the conditions established by those who had ruled before him.

He considered those restrictions dangerous to his purpose and would not allow counsel or opposition to turn him from what he had determined.

He therefore armed himself against those who resisted him.

He drove his opponents from the city, overcame the body of rulers who stood against him, and killed many of them.

Caesar then took control of the Roman state and held its government in his own hand.

From that time he bore the title Caesar.

### Caesar and Pompey

Pompey was still powerful among the Romans when these things took place.

When he learned what Caesar had done, he gathered an army and went out to make war against him.

Caesar came against Pompey, and the two forces met in battle.

Pompey was defeated and fled.

Caesar pursued him, overtook him, and Pompey was killed.

Caesar then took possession of the whole government of Rome.

After this Caesar determined to go into Egypt because many of Pompey's commanders and supporters had gathered there and remained strong.

Before entering Egypt, however, he went first into the land of Syria so that he might secure that region.

### Mithridates Submits to Caesar

When Mithridates, king of the Syrians, learned that Caesar had entered the land, he went out to meet him.

He submitted himself to Caesar and declared his obedience.

Caesar commanded Mithridates to march into Egypt and make war against the commanders and soldiers who remained there from the party of Pompey.

Mithridates obeyed.

He departed from his own land and came as far as Ashkelon.

When Hyrcanus heard of Caesar's victory and learned that he had entered Syria, he became afraid.

Hyrcanus had long been known for his obedience and friendship toward Pompey.

He therefore judged that it was wise to do something by which he might draw near to Caesar and gain his favor.

Hyrcanus sent Antipater with a large army to assist Mithridates.

Antipater joined him at Ashkelon, and Mithridates received him with honor.

The two then marched together toward Egypt.

### Antipater in the Egyptian War

A powerful Egyptian army came out against them.

The fighting was severe.

At first the Egyptians prevailed over Mithridates and surrounded him so that they might destroy him.

Antipater saw his danger and rushed to his aid.

He and his men threw themselves against the Egyptians and fought with great courage.

They broke the forces that had surrounded Mithridates and drove them back.

Mithridates was then able to enter the land and establish control there.

Antipater received many wounds in the battle.

His endurance and courage became widely known because of what he had done.

Mithridates wrote to Caesar and reported the whole matter.

He told him that Antipater had borne the greatest part of the struggle and that his courage had turned the course of the war in Egypt.

When Caesar read the letter, he praised Antipater and sent for him.

Antipater came to Caesar together with Mithridates.

Caesar received him gladly, honored him, and raised him to a place of distinction.

### Antigonus Accuses Hyrcanus and Antipater

At that time Antigonus son of Aristobulus came before Caesar and complained against his uncle Hyrcanus and against Antipater.

He reminded Caesar that his father Aristobulus had been released from Rome and sent out to oppose Pompey.

Antigonus accused Hyrcanus and Antipater of having used deceit against Aristobulus until he was killed.

He presented the death of his father as an act committed because Aristobulus had been sent to serve Caesar against Pompey.

Caesar therefore questioned Antipater concerning the accusation.

Antipater answered:

“I once obeyed Pompey because he commanded the armies of Rome and held authority over us.

“But I have not fought the Egyptians now, nor endured the suffering that has come upon me, in order to repay obedience to a man who is dead.

“What I have done, I have done in obedience to Caesar and in service to him.”

Antipater then uncovered his head and body and showed Caesar the wounds and scars that remained upon him from the fighting.

He said:

“These are witnesses for me.

“They show that my obedience to Caesar and my devotion to him are greater than the obedience I once gave to Pompey.

“Not every man is willing to endure what I have endured in order to stand with Caesar in his wars.”

### Caesar Honors Antipater

When Caesar heard Antipater's words and saw the wounds upon him, he was pleased.

He answered him with kindness and said:

“May peace and safety remain with you.

“Your deeds have made your courage known.

“These marks upon your body bear witness to the sincerity of your loyalty and to the strength of your devotion.

“I therefore place you among my trusted companions and give you authority among my commanders and armies.”

Caesar then told Antipater that he intended to go again toward the eastern lands and wished him to accompany him.

Caesar went out to make war against the Persians, and Antipater went with him.

In that campaign Antipater again showed courage, strength, and faithfulness.

His deeds increased Caesar's affection for him, and Caesar continued to honor him.

When the campaign was finished, Caesar sent Antipater back to his own land in great favor and with greater honor than before.

Caesar then returned to Rome.

### Hyrcanus in Jerusalem

The affairs of Hyrcanus became settled in Jerusalem.

His position grew stronger, and he conducted himself well among the people.

The account remembers him as a good and upright man.

But he was not inclined toward war or toward the command of armies.

He preferred quietness and peace and was known for avoiding the dangers of battle.
