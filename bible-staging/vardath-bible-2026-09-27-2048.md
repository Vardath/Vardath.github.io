### Judah, Tlemias, and Herod

After these things Tlemias gathered one hundred and twenty thousand men and one thousand horsemen and marched against Judah.

Judah went out against him with ten thousand men.

He defeated Tlemias and killed a great number of his soldiers.

Tlemias then humbled himself before Judah and asked him for mercy.

He swore that he would never again make war against him and that he would deal kindly with the Jews who lived throughout the territories under his authority.

Judah had compassion on him and spared his life.

Tlemias kept the oath he had sworn.

Afterward Herod gathered one hundred thousand men from Mount al-Sharāh together with four hundred horsemen and marched against Judah.

In the first fighting Herod killed the commander of Judah’s army and a number of his men.

Then Judah and his companions advanced against him.

They defeated Herod, killed many of his soldiers, and put the rest to flight.

Herod escaped from the battle.

Judah pursued him, but the surviving reading of the place-name connected with the end of the pursuit is uncertain. Another report in the text says that Herod was killed there.

### Eupator Breaks the Covenant

When Eupator learned how strong the Jews had become and heard that Judah had entered into an alliance with Rome, he turned against the agreement that had been made with the Jews and broke his covenant with them.

He marched with Lysias, his kinsman, at the head of a great army into the land of Judah and came against Beth-zur.

When Judah heard of their approach, he gathered his companions, the elders of the nation, and its leaders.

They fasted, prayed, and offered many sacrifices to God.

Then they went out against the Greeks.

Judah fell upon them by night and killed about four thousand men.

After this he returned to his camp.

When morning came, the two armies drew up against one another and the battle became fierce.

Judah saw one of the elephants adorned with golden armor and supposed that the king was riding upon it.

He called out among his men and asked who would go forward and kill the elephant.

A young man named Eleazar came out from among the household.

He pressed into the battle line, striking the soldiers on his right and on his left until the men before him gave way.

Eleazar reached the elephant, went beneath it, and struck it in the belly.

The elephant fell upon him, and Eleazar died beneath it.

When the king saw what had happened, he commanded that the fighting cease.

Among the leading men of the army who fell that day were about eight hundred, besides those killed among the common soldiers and those who had died in the night attack.

At that same time word came to Eupator that a man named Philip had rebelled against him.

He was also told that Demetrius son of Seleucus had departed from Rome and was seeking to recover his kingdom.

When Eupator heard these things, fear increased within him.

He therefore sent to Judah asking for peace, and Judah agreed.

Eupator and Lysias met Judah and made a covenant with him.

They swore that they would not again make war against him and would not return to Jerusalem in hostility.

Eupator gave a large sum of money to the house of God.

Then he departed from the land of Judah and returned toward Macedonia.

Judah returned to Jerusalem and continued in the good order, justice, and care for the welfare of the nation that had marked his rule.

The reason Eupator had broken the earlier covenant with the Jews was Menelaus, one of the three wicked accusers mentioned before.

Menelaus had formerly gone to the first Antiochus and spoken against the Jews.

He now went also to Eupator, stirred him against them, persuaded him to break the covenant, and urged him to make war upon them.

After peace was restored between Eupator and Judah, the king regretted what he had done in breaking the agreement.

He became angry with Menelaus because of his slander.

Eupator ordered that Menelaus’s hands and feet be bound and that he be taken up to a high place and cast down from it.

Thus Menelaus died a miserable death.

The account says that God caused punishment to overtake him in this life according to the evil of his deeds.

By this judgment the king also sought to satisfy the people of Judah, for Menelaus had been one of their greatest enemies and had caused the death of many among them.
