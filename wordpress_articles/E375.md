## Expanded Post E375 — The Five Suns: Successive Worlds Before the Present One

**Categories:** Cosmology; Mexica Tradition; World Ages; Reset

**Excerpt:** The Mexica Five Suns tradition gives Vardath Cosmology one of its clearest world-age comparisons: the present world follows earlier worlds, each associated with a different order and catastrophic ending. The comparison concerns succession and reset, not a claim that the Five Suns literally record Vardath world-turns.

### Final article

Few traditions fit the **succession** side of Vardath Cosmology as clearly as the Mexica accounts commonly grouped under the **Five Suns**.

The important feature is not any one catastrophe.

It is the fact that the present world is **not the first world**.

Earlier ages exist before it.

They end.

Another world follows.

That gives the Vardath atlas an immediately recognisable sequence:

**WORLD 1 → END → WORLD 2 → END → WORLD 3 → END → WORLD 4 → END → PRESENT WORLD.**

This is structurally close to the project’s own mature image:

**previous/lower turn → present turn → next/upper turn**, embedded within a larger sequence of successive stable worlds.

The first principle is:

**A COSMOLOGY CAN TREAT WORLD HISTORY AS SUCCESSIVE STABLE REGIMES RATHER THAN ONE UNBROKEN WORLD.**

That is exactly what Vardath needs if its shell-turn model is to have mythological comparanda stronger than isolated flood stories.

The Five Suns material does not merely say catastrophe happened.

It places catastrophe **between worlds**.

That distinction matters.

A flood inside one world is weather or disaster.

A catastrophe that ends one age and precedes another is a **reset boundary**.

The second feature is that different worlds can have **different conditions and inhabitants**.

Vardath has explored the idea that adjacent turns may differ in atmosphere, biology, scale or environmental regime. Those claims remain speculative.

The Five Suns tradition is useful not because it proves such physical differences, but because it makes world ages **qualitatively distinct**.

The present order is one among several possible orders.

That gives the principle:

**SUCCESSIVE WORLDS NEED NOT BE IDENTICAL COPIES.**

This is central to Vardath.

If each turn were exactly the same, reset would have little meaning.

The model instead expects inherited material arranged under a new stability.

The third feature is **catastrophe as transition marker**.

Different versions and sources associate earlier Suns with different endings, including destructive forces such as wind, fire and water.

Vardath should not force those into one literal mechanism.

The useful comparison is that **different visible catastrophes can occupy the same structural role**.

One world ends through one image.

Another through another.

The invariant is:

**STABLE AGE → DISRUPTION → TERMINATION → SUCCESSOR AGE.**

This supports one of Vardath’s strongest comparative claims:

myths do not need to preserve identical surface effects to belong to the same broad transition grammar.

One culture may remember water.

Another fire.

Another darkness.

Another wind.

If the deeper model involves changing geometry, several kinds of surface consequence could differ from event to event or place to place.

The fourth feature is **counted succession**.

The worlds are not an indefinite fog of previous ages.

They are organised into a sequence.

Vardath should be careful here.

Its own numbers—1, 3, 7, 8, 11, 137 and the various speculative cycle intervals—must not be imposed onto the Five Suns.

The value lies in **ordinal structure**, not numerical equivalence.

There is a before.

There is another before.

There is a present.

The world has depth in time.

That is enough.

The fifth feature is **present-world contingency**.

If there were previous Suns, then the present Sun is not cosmologically inevitable.

It is one achieved state in a larger sequence.

That fits Vardath’s view of the present world as one settled turn.

The present is real and stable enough for ordinary life, but it is not the totality of possible world-order.

This gives the principle:

**THE PRESENT WORLD IS A PHASE, NOT THE WHOLE COSMOS.**

That is one of the deepest points of the Vardath shell model.

The sixth feature is the possibility of a **future ending**.

World-age cosmologies often make the present order finite as well.

Vardath similarly imagines the present stable turn as temporary on cosmological timescales.

The model should remain careful not to assign a date or pretend ancient sources predict a modern event.

But the structural comparison is straightforward:

**if previous worlds ended, the present world belongs to the same class of finite states.**

The seventh feature is **inheritance across ages**.

The Five Suns tradition is not simply a series of disconnected universes.

The ages belong to one cosmological history.

For Vardath, that is crucial.

The previous world, present world and next world are related turns of one larger structure.

Material, memory and pattern can carry forward.

The world changes without the cosmos becoming an entirely unrelated creation each time.

This gives the grammar:

**SUCCESSION WITH CONTINUITY.**

The eighth feature is the difference between **cycle and exact repetition**.

“Cycle” can be misleading if it suggests that the same world returns identically.

The Five Suns material helps correct that.

The ages recur structurally—world, catastrophe, world—but their contents differ.

Vardath should therefore use “cycle” in the same careful sense:

**recurrent transition pattern, not identical replay.**

That is a major conceptual safeguard.

The full Vardath mapping can be written as:

**STABLE WORLD A → CATASTROPHIC TERMINATION → STABLE WORLD B → CATASTROPHIC TERMINATION → STABLE WORLD C … → PRESENT STABLE WORLD.**

Inside the shell model:

**TURN n−1 → TRANSITION → TURN n → TRANSITION → TURN n+1.**

The comparison is powerful because it sits at the right scale.

A serpent can represent a current.

A mountain can represent an axis.

A cave can represent a boundary.

But the Five Suns address the **entire history of worlds**.

That makes them one of the strongest world-age analogies in the whole Vardath atlas.

The tradition does not prove successive physical shell-turns.

It does not validate any Vardath timing number.

It does not establish that the earlier Suns correspond to geological ages.

But it does demonstrate a cosmological imagination in which the world people inhabit is one stable order among several, and catastrophic transitions separate those orders.

That is almost exactly the structural question Vardath Cosmology asks.

What if “the world” is not one permanent object?

What if it is the currently inhabited stable phase of a larger recurring system?

The Five Suns give an ancient answer in mythic form:

there were worlds before this one.

They ended.

This world followed.

And world-history is larger than the world we currently see.
