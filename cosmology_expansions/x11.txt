The Met image is public domain; Cleveland identifies the displayed works as Open Access/Public Domain; Walters supplies CC0 collection imagery; the Urnes photograph is Bjørn Erik Pedersen, CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons.</p><h3>Yggdrasil is not one fixed “Viking symbol”</h3><p>There is no securely established single geometric glyph that can simply be called <strong>the Viking Yggdrasil symbol</strong>. Yggdrasil is a mythological world-tree, and modern commercial symbols labelled “Yggdrasil” are often much later inventions. The useful early material is broader. The Swedish History Museum dates the Överhogdal textiles to roughly 1070–1140 and explicitly notes a Tree-of-Life/world-tree type motif in them. Norse myth itself places nine worlds in Yggdrasil. Meanwhile the Urnes style preserves a different but contemporary visual grammar: an elongated great beast, snake-like forms and thin ribbons woven into sweeping interlace.</p><p>That distinction is useful for the Vardath model rather than inconvenient. If the proposed lattice can expand into a mesh and contract into an axial rod, it should <strong>not</strong> always look like the same emblem. A culture looking at, remembering or diagramming different states and angles could preserve different projections of one structure.</p><div class="projection-grid"><div><b>surface / oblique view</b><span>knot · braid · Celtic/Insular interlace · woven mesh</span></div><div><b>end-on / polar view</b><span>wheel · rosette · star · cross · ring around a hub</span></div><div><b>side / axial view</b><span>world tree · rod · pillar · staff · mountain axis</span></div><div><b>local throat view</b><span>paired serpents · dragons · twin tails · caduceus-like channels</span></div><div><b>convergence view</b><span>multiple paths narrowing toward one eye, node or central crossing</span></div><div><b>closed / stable boundary</b><span>circle · egg · mandorla · enclosure · eye</span></div></div><p>Within the hypothesis, Yggdrasil is therefore most naturally an <strong>axial/side view</strong> of the system, while Celtic knotwork and Viking animal interlace preserve more of the <strong>surface or oblique mesh</strong>. The Ethiopian wheel-cross and eight-point textile are closer to an <strong>end-on convergence view</strong>. The Moche paired serpents, Chinese interlacing dragons and Aztec knotted snake look more like <strong>local current channels inside the mesh</strong>. This is a Vardath geometric interpretation, not the historical meaning assigned to all of these objects by their cultures.</p><h3>A new prediction from the “different angles” idea</h3><p>This produces a stronger test than simple resemblance. If these symbol families are projections of one underlying three-dimensional grammar, then a large image corpus should cluster into a limited set of related geometric classes—mesh, axial, radial/end-on, paired-channel and enclosure—and intermediate objects should combine neighbouring classes in predictable ways. A free-form symbolic theory could match anything; a projection model should instead constrain what combinations and transitions are common.</p><p>For example, the model now predicts that <strong>paired-serpent imagery should preferentially occur in interlaced or axial compositions; radial wheel/star forms should preferentially carry a central node; and tree/rod compositions should more often acquire paired flanking, winding or branching forms than matched non-axial controls.</strong> These can be tested independently on images without using mythology labels at all.</p></div>
