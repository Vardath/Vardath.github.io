<h2>Water horse / Ceffyl Dŵr / Sleipnir — source and language boundaries</h2>
<p><strong>This note is the assistant’s voice.</strong> The main section deliberately develops Stephen’s structural reading. The linguistic point needs to stay separate: Welsh <strong>ceffyl dŵr</strong> means <strong>water horse</strong> — <em>ceffyl</em> is horse and <em>dŵr</em> is water. “World door” is therefore a Vardath phonetic/symbolic reinterpretation of the role, not a historical Welsh etymology.</p>
<p>The Ceffyl Dŵr material itself does support the specific folklore features used above: association with pools, waterfalls, rivers and seashores; horse form; regional luminous/winged descriptions; very rapid transport; and stories in which the mount rises into sky or mist and disappears. The wider water-horse family also genuinely includes Welsh, Scottish, Irish, Manx and Nordic forms, although those traditions should not be collapsed into one original creature without historical evidence.</p>
<p>Sleipnir is also a real functional comparison rather than a taxonomic one. Norse sources identify Sleipnir as Odin’s eight-legged horse and as a mount capable of travel associated with Hel; Hermóðr rides Sleipnir on the road to Hel after Baldr’s death. But Sleipnir is not conventionally classified as a Ceffyl Dŵr, kelpie or each-uisge. The Vardath comparison is therefore <strong>role-based: extraordinary horse + boundary crossing + transport between normally separated regions</strong>.</p>
<p>The historical images are useful as reception evidence, not eyewitness evidence. Kittelsen’s water-spirit horse is from 1909, Thomas Millie Dow’s <em>Kelpie</em> is from 1895, Draper’s <em>Kelpie</em> is from 1913, and the Icelandic Sleipnir illustration is from an eighteenth-century manuscript. They show how later artists pictured the traditions, not what an ancient observer literally saw.</p>
<p>A stronger future test could freeze the sequence <strong>horse/mount → water or liminal boundary → rider attachment → abnormal transport → disappearance/other-realm arrival</strong> and compare it across a curated Celtic/Nordic folklore corpus against ordinary horse tales and ordinary water-spirit tales. That would test whether the complete vehicle-at-boundary package is actually enriched rather than assembled from famous examples.</p>
