science actually shows</h2><p>Fired clay does undergo real thermal changes. Quartz has an α→β
inversion around 573 °C that produces a measurable length anomaly.
Experimental clay specimens can show soft thermal expansion up to a
maximum around the 700–760 °C range. However, one published study found
maximum expansions on the order of roughly <strong>0.5–1.13%</strong>,
followed by shrinkage as vitreous phases formed above roughly 780–800
°C. Other brick studies likewise use quartz inversion and residual
shrinkage as indicators of firing history.</p><p>That does <strong>not</strong> support ordinary bricks swelling into
mountain-sized blocks. It instead gives the old hypothesis a much
sharper test:</p><ul>
<li>Are claimed “expanded bricks” chemically and mineralogically
consistent with fired clay or masonry?</li>
<li>Do they contain melt glass, vitrified surfaces, vesicles,
recrystallized phases or former mortar boundaries?</li>
<li>Are crystal fabrics consistent with rapid thermal processing?</li>
<li>Can measured phase-volume changes explain the claimed scale, or
would enormous additional porosity/gas expansion be required?</li>
<li>Is there an energy source capable of heating the required mass?</li>
<li>Do geologic ages and stratigraphy agree with a recent architectural
origin, or contradict it?</li>
</ul>
